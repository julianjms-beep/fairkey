    Can you trust it?

    We judged FairKey the way a careful lender or regulator would: on applications from a year it had never seen, against simple rules people already use.

    Finding the denials without crying wolf

    Precision–recall on 2025 applications. Higher is better: catch more denials while warning fewer families who would have been approved.

    When it says 30%, is it 30%?

    Each dot groups 2025 applications by predicted risk. On the line = honest probabilities a person can act on.

    Why not accuracy?

    The leak we refused

    HMDA records an interest rate only for loans that were made. A model allowed to look at it is "99.98% accurate" — and useless, because you don't have a rate before you apply. The same with "pre-approved": denied pre-approvals are filed under another code, so all 740,803 pre-approved applications in the data were approved. And we set aside the 15% of records with blanks: 41% of them were denials, so a model would learn that "the lender never filled this in" means "no".

    The same model, to the decimal

    The model in your browser is the exact one we tested. On 500 test applications our JavaScript engine matches LightGBM's own predictions within 0.000002 and its SHAP explanations within 0.000001.

    The fairness audit

    FairKey never sees race, ethnicity, sex or age. But being blind is not the same as being fair — other inputs can stand in for them. So we checked, on 2025 applications it had never seen.

    Expected = what FairKey predicts from finances alone. Ratio above 1 = denied more often than finances explain (95% bootstrap interval). Wrongly flagged = approved families FairKey had marked "high risk". Caught = denials FairKey had marked "high risk".

    For the same predicted risk, who is denied more?

    Is it the same in every state?

    One square per state. The number compares Black (or Hispanic) and White applicants FairKey rated the same, in 2025: ×1.5 means they were denied 1.5 times as often, beyond what their finances explain. Tap a state.

    Can the inputs reveal race?

    We trained a second model to guess whether an applicant is Black or Hispanic (vs. White) from each set of inputs. 0.5 = no better than a coin; 1.0 = perfect.

    Which version did we ship?

    We trained five versions on the same years and compared accuracy with how often each one wrongly flagged approved families (Black · Hispanic · White).

    How it works

    Every step is in the open notebook and runs again from public data.

    Code on GitHubOpen the notebook in Colab

    1. Real decisionsEvery home-purchase application in the 50 states and DC that lenders reported to the U.S. government (HMDA), 2022–2025: {nAll} million.
    2. Leak checkRemove anything known only after the decision (interest rate, underwriting system) and records with blanks.
    3. LearnGradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) learn from 2022–2023, with a built-in guarantee: more debt or a smaller down payment can never lower the risk.
    4. Calibrate2024 tunes the model and turns scores into honest probabilities.
    5. Test once2025 applications, never touched before, against a 43% debt rule and logistic regression.
    6. AuditRace, sex and age gaps, a proxy detector, and a neighbourhood model we chose not to ship.
    7. Your browserThe trees, TreeSHAP explanations, the reason model and the what-if search all run on your device.

    Where the AI is

    • Risk model — {nRisk} boosted trees predict the chance of denial from {nFeat} inputs.
    • Reason model — {nReason} trees predict which of six reasons a lender would give.
    • Explanations — exact TreeSHAP splits every prediction into the part each answer caused.
    • What-if search — the model tests realistic changes and ranks them.
    • Fairness audit — observed vs. expected outcomes, error rates, and a proxy-detector model.

    Where it fails

    • No credit scores in public data — the biggest thing a lender sees that FairKey can't.
    • Home purchases only, first mortgages on homes people will live in; no Puerto Rico or other territories.
    • Debt-to-income here is our estimate; the lender computes its own.
    • It learns from past decisions, including any unfairness in them. The audit measures that; it cannot remove it from the world.
    • A prediction, not a promise: in the "likely yes" band, {lowDen} in 100 were still denied.
